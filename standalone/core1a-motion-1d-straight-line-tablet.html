MIC-PHY-KIN-DISTANCE-DISPLACEMENT

1 · Path travelled is not start-to-finish change

R1 · Default

Predict

You walk 30 m east, then 30 m west back to the marker. Has every measure of the journey returned to zero?

Model the two jobs

Distance keeps the whole travelled route. Displacement keeps only the directed change from start to finish. A return trip can therefore have nonzero distance and zero displacement.

Math bridge

distance = Σ |segment length|    ·    displacement = xf − xi

Misconception repair

Wrong: “They are always the same number.” Repair: compare two routes with the same travelled length but different endpoints.

Worked anchor

30 m east + 30 m west → distance = 60 m. Finish = start → displacement = 0 m.

Try before reveal

Route A returns to its marker. Route B travels the same total amount but ends 20 m east. Compare distance and displacement.

Check your transfer
Equal travelled amount can mean equal distance. Route A has zero displacement; Route B has a nonzero eastward displacement.
MIC-PHY-KIN-AVERAGE-RATES

2 · Average rates use different totals

R2 · Default

Predict

A cyclist goes 100 m out and 100 m back in 40 s. Can average speed be nonzero while average velocity is zero?

Model

Average speed divides total path length by total time. Average velocity divides signed displacement by the same total time. Do not average listed speeds unless the time/distance weighting actually justifies it.

Math bridge

average speed = total distance / total time
average velocity = displacement / total time

Worked anchor

200 m total distance in 40 s → average speed = 5 m/s. Displacement = 0 → average velocity = 0 m/s.

Boundary

If two equal time intervals use speeds 8 m/s and 12 m/s in the same direction, the arithmetic mean is justified. Equal distances are a different weighting problem: return to total distance / total time.

Transfer

Half a journey is at 12 m/s and half at 18 m/s. Explain why simply averaging 12 and 18 is not justified by “half the journey.”

Check your reasoning
“Half the journey” means equal distances, not equal times. The slower half consumes more time, so compute the total time for the two equal distances.
MIC-PHY-KIN-ZERO-V-NONZERO-A

3 · Zero velocity at an instant does not decide acceleration

R3 · Diagnostic extension

Predict

At the top of a vertical throw, velocity is momentarily zero. Does acceleration also have to be zero?

Three-moment model

Velocity and acceleration answer different questions. Velocity can be zero at one instant while nonzero acceleration continues to change that velocity through the instant.

Contrast

An object resting on a shelf can also have v = 0, but its neighboring moments need not show any velocity change. So v = 0 alone cannot determine a.

Transfer

Describe the velocity just before, at, and just after the highest point of a vertical throw, then state what that sequence tells you about acceleration.

Check
Upward → zero → downward. The velocity is changing downward through the top; with upward positive, gravitational acceleration remains negative.
MIC-PHY-KIN-MOTION-GRAPHS

4 · Graph height and graph slope answer different questions

R4G · Default

Model

On a position–time graph, segment slope is average velocity. On a velocity–time graph, segment slope is average acceleration. Always read axes before interpreting a slope.

Worked anchor

Position changes from 4 m to 16 m between 2 s and 6 s. Segment slope = (16−4)/(6−2) = 3 m/s → average velocity over that interval.

Misconception repair

A high point on a graph is not automatically “fast.” On an x–t graph, height is position; speed/velocity information comes from slope.

Transfer

A velocity–time graph rises from 2 m/s at 1 s to 10 m/s at 5 s. What does the segment slope mean and what is its value?

Check
Slope = (10−2)/(5−1) = 2 m/s², which is the average acceleration over that interval.
MIC-PHY-KIN-CONSTANT-ACCELERATION

5 · One straight v–t segment carries the three Grade-9 equations

R4 · Default

Condition first

Use the standard kinematic equations exactly only when one constant acceleration describes the interval. On a v–t graph that means a straight segment.

Derive, don’t merely list

Slope: a = (v−u)/t → v = u + at.
Rectangle + triangle area: s = ut + ½(v−u)t → substitute v−u = at → s = ut + ½at².
Trapezoid area: s = ½(u+v)t and t = (v−u)/a → eliminate t → v² = u² + 2as.

Boundary

If the v–t graph is curved, acceleration is changing. These constant-a equations are then not exact over the whole interval.

Worked anchor

u = 4 m/s, a = 2 m/s², t = 3 s. Find v and s, then check the graph meaning.

Check
v = 4 + 2×3 = 10 m/s. s = 4×3 + ½×2×3² = 21 m. The same 21 m is the area under the straight v–t segment.
MIC-PHY-KIN-UNIFORM-CIRCULAR-MOTION

6 · Constant speed can still mean changing velocity

R5 · Default

Model

In uniform circular motion, speed can stay constant while velocity direction changes continuously. Velocity is tangent to the circle; acceleration points inward. One-revolution speed is circumference divided by period.

Math bridge

v = 2πr / T

Boundary

Default Grade-9 R5 stops at tangent velocity, inward acceleration qualitatively, and speed from circumference/period. The force-role equation belongs to the explicit R6 extension below.

Transfer

A point moves in a circle of radius 2 m once every 4 s. Find its speed and explain why its velocity is not constant.

Check
v = 2π(2)/4 = π m/s. Its speed is constant, but the tangent direction changes continuously, so velocity changes.
Owner extension · non-default — requires the second-law force model. Do not treat this as ordinary Grade-9 completion scope.

MIC-PHY-KIN-CIRCULAR-DYNAMICS-ROLE
R6 · “Centripetal” is a net radial role, not an extra force

The inward acceleration requirement v²/r fixes only the required net radial component mv²/r. Tension, friction, gravity, normal force, or combinations may supply that resultant; never add a separate “centripetal force” interaction.
choose inward-positive radial axis → draw real interactions only → ΣFr = m v²/r
Wrong: draw tension/friction/gravity and then add a new centripetal-force arrow. Repair: “centripetal” names the inward resultant required by the motion, not a new interaction type.

Extension transfer

A car rounds a flat curve. Which real interaction can supply the inward resultant? What happens if the required radial force exceeds that interaction’s available capacity?

Check extension reasoning
Static friction can provide the inward resultant on a flat curve. If the required inward force exceeds available static friction, the no-slip circular-motion model cannot be maintained.
