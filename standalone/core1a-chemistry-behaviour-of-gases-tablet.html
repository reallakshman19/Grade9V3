The key step

Temperature describes ensemble kinetic behaviour rather than one common molecular speed, while phase-change claims require the relevant pressure and equilibrium conditions.

You need

  • Can distinguish temperature from the speed of one molecule and can describe pressure as an external condition on a liquid surface.
  1. Separate the microscopic particle model from the macroscopic observation.
    Why valid: Bulk temperature and pressure summarize many-particle behaviour; one molecule is not the thermodynamic state.
    Result: Use distributions and averages when connecting molecular motion to temperature.
  2. Compare evaporation with boiling before discussing temperature.
    Why valid: Evaporation can occur at a surface over a range of temperatures; boiling is a bulk transition when vapour pressure matches external pressure.
    Result: Pext ↓ ⇒ Tb ↓ because Pvap(Tb) = Pext is reached at a lower vapour pressure; surface evaporation can still occur below Tb.
  3. Track energy through a phase change for a pure substance at fixed pressure in phase equilibrium.
    Why valid: Energy supplied during coexistence can change phase rather than raise temperature.
    Result: At phase coexistence under fixed Pext: energy in > 0 while T = Tb and liquid fraction ↓; the added energy changes phase instead of producing ΔT.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
⟨Eₖ⟩ = 3kBT/2; Pvap(Tb) = Pext

Temperature sets the ensemble mean translational kinetic energy in the ideal-gas model, while bulk boiling begins when vapour pressure reaches the external pressure.

  • The kinetic-energy relation is for an equilibrium ideal monatomic-gas translational model.
  • The boiling criterion refers to a pure liquid at a stated external pressure and does not describe surface evaporation.

Watch one

Why can water boil at a lower temperature at high altitude than near sea level?

  1. Boiling occurs when the liquid's vapour pressure matches the external pressure.
  2. At high altitude the external pressure is lower, so the equality is reached at a lower temperature.

Result

Lower external pressure lets the vapour-pressure equality required for boiling be reached at a lower temperature.

Check

The explanation must use pressure equilibrium rather than a change in water's chemical identity.

Temperature describes ensemble kinetic behaviour rather than one common molecular speed, while phase-change claims require the relevant pressure and equilibrium conditions.

A tempting wrong path

  • All molecules in a thermal sample have the same speed, and a liquid boils at one universal temperature.

Diagnose

  • What would a molecular speed distribution mean if every molecule had one speed, and why does changing external pressure change the boiling condition?

Repair

  • Keep a distribution of molecular energies, and attach external pressure to any boiling-temperature statement.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckWhy does one equilibrium temperature not imply one molecular speed?
  2. 2 · ApplyExternal pressure is lowered. Using Pvap(Tb) = Pext, explain why the boiling temperature falls.
  3. 3 · ConnectCarry the habit of naming the model condition into gas-law and phase-equilibrium questions.

The key step

Boyle, Charles, Gay-Lussac, Avogadro, Dalton and Graham relations answer different conditional questions; the variables and observable must be named before selecting a relation.

You need

  • Can identify pressure, volume, amount and absolute temperature as distinct state variables and can read direct and inverse proportionality statements.
  1. Name pressure, volume, absolute temperature and amount, then state which variables are fixed.
    Why valid: A proportionality is valid only on the constrained path for which it was derived.
    Result: Constraints come before substitution.
  2. Use the ideal-gas equation as a common model for state-variable changes.
    Why valid: For a fixed amount of ideal gas, changing one state variable generally requires compensating changes in others.
    Result: For fixed n and T: P1V1 = P2V2; if V2 = V1/2, then P2 = 2P1.
  3. Separate Dalton partial-pressure bookkeeping from Graham effusion-rate comparison.
    Why valid: Dalton adds component partial pressures in an ideal mixture; Graham compares transport rates under its effusion assumptions.
    Result: Molar mass belongs in the rate comparison, not in the ideal partial-pressure sum.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
PV=nRT

The ideal-gas equation links pressure, volume, amount and absolute temperature; simple gas-law proportionalities are constrained cases of this state model.

  • Use an ideal-gas model or a regime where ideal behaviour is an adequate approximation.
  • State which variables are held fixed before reducing the equation to a simple proportionality.

Watch one

A fixed amount of ideal gas is compressed to half its volume at constant temperature. What happens to its pressure?

  1. Amount and temperature are fixed.
  2. Under this isothermal constraint pressure is inversely proportional to volume.
  3. Halving the volume therefore doubles the pressure.

Result

Its pressure doubles in the ideal-gas model.

Check

Verify that pressure times volume is unchanged between the two states.

Boyle, Charles, Gay-Lussac, Avogadro, Dalton and Graham relations answer different conditional questions; the variables and observable must be named before selecting a relation.

A tempting wrong path

  • Any familiar gas-law proportion can be used when several supposedly fixed variables are also changing.

Diagnose

  • If temperature and volume both change, can a fixed-temperature Boyle-law relation alone determine the final pressure?

Repair

  • Write the constraints first and use the combined or ideal-gas model when more than one state variable changes.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckBefore using Boyle's law, which variables must be fixed?
  2. 2 · ApplyFor fixed n and T, volume is halved. Use PV = nRT to determine the pressure change.
  3. 3 · ConnectUse the ideal state as the reference before discussing real-gas deviation with Z.

Try it with less support

Why does Graham's law not justify assigning a larger partial pressure to a lighter gas solely because it has lower molar mass?

The key step

Real-gas deviations matter when neglected finite-size and intermolecular-interaction effects are no longer small; equation-of-state corrections do not uniquely specify a microscopic mechanism.

You need

  • Can use the ideal-gas equation as a reference model and can distinguish a thermodynamic state relation from a microscopic mechanism.
  1. Use compressibility factor as a comparison with the ideal reference at the same state.
    Why valid: Deviation has meaning relative to a pressure-temperature state and an ideal reference.
    Result: Z = 1 is the ideal reference; the sign and size of deviation depend on conditions.
  2. Interpret van der Waals attraction and excluded-volume terms as phenomenological corrections.
    Why valid: Attractions and finite molecular size affect the pressure-volume relation differently and with state-dependent importance.
    Result: Z ≠ 1 ⇒ the stated P,V,n,T state deviates from the ideal reference; Z alone does not identify one unique microscopic mechanism.
  3. Distinguish a critical point from an ordinary boiling point.
    Why valid: The critical point terminates liquid-vapour coexistence; above the critical temperature compression alone cannot create a separate liquid phase.
    Result: Liquefaction claims require the temperature-pressure regime to be stated.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
Z=PVnRT

The compressibility factor compares a measured real-gas state with the ideal-gas reference at the same P, V, n and T.

  • Compare states using consistent P, V, n and T values.
  • Interpret the sign and magnitude of deviation only for the stated thermodynamic state.

Watch one

Why are real-gas deviations generally more important at high pressure than at sufficiently low pressure?

  1. The ideal model neglects molecular volume and intermolecular forces.
  2. Closer average separation makes those neglected effects comparatively more important.

Result

At high pressure molecules are closer together, so finite molecular size and intermolecular interactions become less negligible relative to ideal assumptions.

Check

Identify the neglected ideal assumptions without claiming every gas deviates in the same direction.

Real-gas deviations matter when neglected finite-size and intermolecular-interaction effects are no longer small; equation-of-state corrections do not uniquely specify a microscopic mechanism.

A tempting wrong path

  • A van der Waals equation proves the exact molecular collision mechanism and gives one universal direction of deviation.

Diagnose

  • Does an equation of state uniquely determine how molecules collide, and can attraction and finite-size effects dominate equally at every pressure and temperature?

Repair

  • Treat the correction parameters as state-model terms and interpret deviations only with the stated thermodynamic conditions.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckWhat does Z = 1 mean, and what does it not tell you about molecular mechanism?
  2. 2 · ApplyAt high pressure, explain why finite molecular size and intermolecular interactions can no longer be neglected.
  3. 3 · ConnectRelate model deviation to the separate liquid-vapour boundary and critical-point idea.
