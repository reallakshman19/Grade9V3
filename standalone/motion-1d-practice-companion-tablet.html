T1 · Reversal path

distance vs displacementrepresentationsign convention
A robot moves +7 m, then −11 m, then +2 m along one straight line. Find its displacement and total distance travelled.
Key concept: displacement depends only on start and finish; distance counts every leg. Put all three legs on one signed axis before calculating.

Worked solution

Understand

Keep two different questions separate: net change in position and accumulated path length.

Represent

Take right as +x. Start at x = 0. After +7 m the robot is at +7 m; after −11 m it is at −4 m; after +2 m it is at −2 m.

Calculate

displacement = (+7) + (−11) + (+2) = −2 m
distance = |7| + |−11| + |2| = 20 m

Interpret

The displacement is 2 m in the negative direction; the robot travelled 20 m in total.

Independent check: the final coordinate is −2 m, so the displacement must be −2 m regardless of the route.

Common wrong route: adding signed legs and calling the result “distance”. Distance cannot be negative and must include all path lengths.

T2 · Round-trip averages

average speedaverage velocitytotal time
A vehicle travels 60 km to a station at 40 km/h and returns the same 60 km at 30 km/h. Find the average speed and average velocity for the complete trip.
Representation: draw the out-and-back route. The vehicle returns to its start, but the travel times of the two legs are different.

Worked solution

Understand

Average speed uses total distance; average velocity uses displacement. Both use the same total elapsed time.

Calculate the times

t_out = 60/40 = 1.5 h; t_back = 60/30 = 2 h
total time = 3.5 h; total distance = 120 km
average speed = 120/3.5 = 34.2857… km/h ≈ 34.3 km/h

The final position equals the initial position, so displacement = 0 km.

average velocity = 0/3.5 = 0 km/h

Independent check: the average speed must lie between 30 and 40 km/h and should be closer to 30 because more time is spent on the slower leg.

Common wrong route: using (40 + 30)/2 = 35 km/h. That simple average would require equal times, not equal distances.

T3 · Negative velocity and negative acceleration

signed velocityaccelerationspeed change
A particle has u = −4 m/s and constant a = −3 m/s² for 2 s. Find its final velocity and decide whether its speed increased or decreased.
First move: use v = u + at. Then compare magnitudes |u| and |v|; do not equate “negative acceleration” with “slowing down”.

Worked solution

Keep the sign convention fixed. The initial velocity and acceleration both point in the negative direction.

v = u + at = −4 + (−3)(2) = −10 m/s

Initial speed = 4 m/s; final speed = 10 m/s. Therefore the particle speeds up.

Independent check: acceleration and velocity have the same sign throughout this interval, so the magnitude of velocity should increase.

Common wrong route: reading “negative acceleration” as a synonym for deceleration. Whether speed rises or falls depends on the relative directions of velocity and acceleration.

T4 · Choose the no-time equation

model choiceconstant accelerationequation selection
A car moves at 5 m/s and accelerates uniformly at 2 m/s² through 24 m. Find its final speed without finding time.
First mathematical move: time is neither given nor wanted. Under constant acceleration, choose the relation connecting u, v, a and s directly.

Worked solution

Model check

“Accelerates uniformly” permits the constant-acceleration relations over the stated 24 m interval.

v² = u² + 2as
v² = 5² + 2(2)(24) = 25 + 96 = 121
v = 11 m/s

Choose the positive root because the question asks for speed and the car is continuing in the positive direction.

Independent check: positive acceleration over positive displacement should make the speed larger than 5 m/s; 11 m/s is consistent.

Common wrong route: first solving for time just because v = u + at is familiar. Correct equation choice can remove an unnecessary unknown.

T5 · Read slope from an x–t graph

position–timesloperest interval
An x–t graph rises linearly from x = 2 m at t = 0 to x = 14 m at t = 4 s, then remains flat for 3 s. Find the velocity in each interval.
Representation: on an x–t graph, velocity is the slope Δx/Δt. A horizontal segment has zero slope even if its vertical coordinate is not zero.

Worked solution

v₁ = Δx/Δt = (14 − 2)/(4 − 0) = 12/4 = 3 m/s

From t = 4 s to t = 7 s, x is constant.

v₂ = Δx/Δt = 0/3 = 0 m/s

Independent check: a straight rising x–t segment means constant positive velocity; a flat segment means the object is at rest.

Common wrong route: reading x = 14 m as a velocity. Graph height gives position; slope gives velocity.

T6 · Signed area under a v–t graph

velocity–timesigned areadistance
A particle moves at +4 m/s for 3 s, then at −2 m/s for 4 s. Find total displacement and total distance.
Key concept: signed area under v–t gives displacement. Distance uses the magnitude of each interval’s displacement.

Worked solution

Δx₁ = (+4)(3) = +12 m
Δx₂ = (−2)(4) = −8 m
total displacement = +12 − 8 = +4 m
total distance = |12| + |−8| = 20 m

Independent check: distance must be at least the magnitude of displacement; 20 m ≥ 4 m.

Common wrong route: adding the two areas without preserving sign for displacement, or preserving sign when calculating distance.

T7 · Three-phase motion

piecewise motionv–t areaphase boundary
A cart accelerates uniformly from 0 to 8 m/s in 4 s, travels at 8 m/s for 5 s, then brakes uniformly to rest in 2 s. Find the total displacement.
Representation: sketch v against t. The three phases form a triangle, rectangle and triangle. Their total area is displacement.

Worked solution

The acceleration law changes between phases, so do not force one constant-a equation across the entire 11 s.

s₁ = ½(4)(8) = 16 m
s₂ = (5)(8) = 40 m
s₃ = ½(2)(8) = 8 m
s_total = 16 + 40 + 8 = 64 m

Independent check: average velocity over the 11 s is 64/11 ≈ 5.82 m/s, which is plausible because velocity stays between 0 and 8 m/s.

Common wrong route: calculating one acceleration for the whole journey despite the cruise interval and braking interval.

T8 · Highest point in vertical motion

vertical motionevent conditiongravity
A ball is thrown vertically upward at 18 m/s. Take g = 9 m/s² and upward as positive. Find the time to the highest point.
Event condition: at the highest point the instantaneous velocity is zero. Gravity is still −9 m/s² there.

Worked solution

v = u + at
0 = 18 + (−9)t
t = 2 s

Independent check: velocity decreases by 9 m/s each second, so 18 m/s takes 2 s to fall to zero.

Common wrong route: setting acceleration to zero at the top. Velocity is momentarily zero; gravitational acceleration is not.

T9 · Free-fall time scaling

scalingfree fallfrom rest
Two stones are dropped from rest under the same constant gravitational acceleration. One falls for time t and the other for time 2t. Compare the distances fallen.
First move: for a drop from rest under constant g, s = ½gt². Compare the time factors before inserting any numbers.

Worked solution

s₁ = ½gt²
s₂ = ½g(2t)² = ½g(4t²) = 4s₁

The second stone falls four times the distance.

Independent check: constant acceleration from rest gives quadratic, not linear, growth of distance with time.

Common wrong route: doubling the time and therefore doubling the distance. That would fit constant velocity, not acceleration from rest.

T10 · Relative acceleration in the same gravitational field

relative motioncommon accelerationframe
Two objects are simultaneously in ideal free fall near Earth and experience the same gravitational acceleration g in the same inertial frame. What is their relative acceleration?
Representation: relative acceleration is the vector difference a_rel = a_B − a_A. Both absolute accelerations are the same vector.

Worked solution

Take upward as positive. Both objects have acceleration −g.

a_rel = a_B − a_A = (−g) − (−g) = 0

The relative velocity therefore remains constant while the shared-g idealisation holds.

Independent check: identical accelerations change both velocities by the same amount in the same time, so their velocity difference does not change.

Boundary: this conclusion relies on both objects experiencing the same g in the same frame; appreciably different fields or drag would break the assumption.

T11 · Equal-distance average speed

average speedharmonic structureequal distance
A car covers two equal 1 km legs at 20 m/s and 30 m/s. Find its average speed.
Key concept: the distances are equal, not the times. Compute the time of each leg and use total distance divided by total time.

Worked solution

Let each leg be L = 1000 m.

t₁ = 1000/20 = 50 s; t₂ = 1000/30 = 33.333… s
v_avg = 2000/(50 + 33.333…) = 24 m/s

Independent check: 24 m/s lies between 20 and 30 and is closer to 20 because the slower leg occupies more time.

Common wrong route: using (20 + 30)/2 = 25 m/s. That would be the average for equal time spent at each speed.

T12 · Displacement during the fifth second

interval vs cumulativeconstant accelerationdifference of states
Starting from rest with constant acceleration, a particle travels 32 m in the first 4 s. Find the displacement during the fifth second.
Representation: “during the fifth second” means the interval from t = 4 s to t = 5 s. Find S(5) − S(4), not S(5).

Worked solution

From rest, S(t) = ½at².

32 = ½a(4²) = 8a ⇒ a = 4 m/s²
S(5) = ½(4)(25) = 50 m
displacement during 5th second = S(5) − S(4) = 50 − 32 = 18 m

Independent check: the object is speeding up, so the distance in the fifth one-second interval should exceed that in earlier one-second intervals.

Common wrong route: reporting 50 m, which is the cumulative displacement after 5 s rather than the displacement only during the fifth second.

T13 · Delayed-start catch-up

shared clockhead startmeeting event
Car A passes a point at 10 m/s. Two seconds later, car B starts from rest at that point with constant acceleration 4 m/s². When does B catch A, measured from B’s start?
First move: reset the clock when B starts. At that instant A is already 20 m ahead. At the catch event both cars have the same position in this new clock.

Worked solution

At τ = 0 (B starts), A has travelled 10×2 = 20 m.

x_A = 20 + 10τ
x_B = ½(4)τ² = 2τ²

Catch-up requires x_A = x_B:

2τ² = 20 + 10τ ⇒ τ² − 5τ − 10 = 0
τ = (5 ± √65)/2

Only the positive time is physical:

τ = (5 + √65)/2 ≈ 6.53 s

Independent check: at 6.53 s, A is about 85.3 m from the origin and B’s 2τ² is also about 85.3 m.

Common wrong route: using the same zero-position initial condition for both cars when B starts, thereby erasing A’s 20 m head start.

T14 · Can an x–t graph loop back on itself?

graph validityfunction of timemodel boundary
A proposed position–time graph forms a closed loop so that, for some time values, one vertical line crosses the curve twice. Can it describe one particle in one frame? Explain.
Boundary test: ask how many positions one particle may occupy at one instant in one frame. Then apply the vertical-line test with time on the horizontal axis.

Worked solution

No. In a classical one-particle description in one frame, each instant t must correspond to one position x(t). If a vertical line at a single time intersects the graph twice, the graph assigns two positions to the same particle at the same instant.

This does not mean a particle cannot reverse direction. A valid x–t graph may rise, flatten and fall; reversal changes the sign of slope but still leaves exactly one x value at each t.

Independent check: apply the vertical-line test with t on the horizontal axis. A valid single-particle x(t) history is single-valued.

Common wrong route: thinking that “looping back in space” requires a loop in x–t. Spatial reversal is shown by changing slope, not by giving one time two positions.
