Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry / Mole Concept / Stoichiometry

Learning promise

Convert between mass, amount and named entities; build empirical ratios; use balanced coefficients for reaction inventory; and reason from concentration denominators.

Concept route

  1. Combination laws, mole, Avogadro constant and molar mass
    1. Empirical combination laws constrain chemical bookkeeping, while the mole links a specified number of entities to measurable mass through Avogadro's constant and molar mass.
  2. Empirical formula, stoichiometry and limiting reagent
    1. Formula subscripts and balanced-equation coefficients are particle/mole ratios, so mass data must first be converted to amount before formula or limiting-reagent decisions are made.
  3. Molarity, molality and mole fraction
    1. Molarity, molality and mole fraction normalize composition differently; a temperature change can alter a volume-based ratio even when the chemical composition of a closed sample is unchanged.

Model contract

  • Name the counted entity—atoms, molecules, ions or formula units—whenever using the mole.
  • Carry units through mass–amount–particle conversions.
  • For concentration, distinguish solution-volume, solvent-mass and mole-ratio denominators and state whether sample composition is unchanged.

Combination laws, mole, Avogadro constant and molar mass

The key step

Empirical combination laws constrain chemical bookkeeping, while the mole links a specified number of entities to measurable mass through Avogadro's constant and molar mass.

You need

  • Can distinguish an empirical mass observation from a particle-model explanation and can perform unit-carrying mass-to-amount conversions.
  1. Separate an empirical combination law from a particle model used to explain it.
    Why valid: A measured mass relation and an atomic interpretation are different kinds of claim.
    Result: The model can explain the regularity without becoming identical to the observation.
  2. Name the counted entity and distinguish moles from grams.
    Why valid: A mole is an amount of specified entities, not one universal mass.
    Result: The mass of one mole depends on the substance's molar mass.
  3. Use the path mass ↔ moles ↔ entities with units visible.
    Why valid: Molar mass connects grams and moles; Avogadro's constant connects moles and entity count.
    Result: 18.0 g H₂O ÷ 18.0 g mol⁻¹ = 1.00 mol H₂O; 1.00 mol × NA = NA water molecules.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
n=mM;N=nNA

Molar mass converts measurable mass to amount of substance, and Avogadro constant converts that amount to a count of explicitly named entities.

  • Name the substance and the entity being counted.
  • Use a molar mass that corresponds to that same chemical entity/formula unit.

Watch one

Why must a particle-count answer specify molecules, atoms, ions or formula units?

  1. Avogadro's constant maps moles to a number of entities.
  2. The substance and chemical representation determine what those entities are.
  3. For an ionic solid, formula units are not the same entity as individual ions.

Result

Because the mole counts specified entities, and different entity choices correspond to different physical counts.

Check

Write the entity name immediately after the numerical particle count.

Empirical combination laws constrain chemical bookkeeping, while the mole links a specified number of entities to measurable mass through Avogadro's constant and molar mass.

A tempting wrong path

  • One mole of every substance has the same mass, and a mole count is meaningful without naming the entity.

Diagnose

  • Do one mole of helium atoms and one mole of water molecules have the same entity count, the same mass, or both?

Repair

  • Keep amount and mass separate, and attach an entity label to every particle-count statement.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckWhat is the same for one mole of any entity, and what generally differs between substances?
  2. 2 · ApplyConvert 0.50 mol H₂O to a molecule count in terms of NA, naming the counted entity.
  3. 3 · ConnectUse moles—not raw masses—as the quantities compared by stoichiometric coefficients.

Empirical formula, stoichiometry and limiting reagent

The key step

Formula subscripts and balanced-equation coefficients are particle/mole ratios, so mass data must first be converted to amount before formula or limiting-reagent decisions are made.

You need

  • Can divide mass by molar or atomic mass to obtain amount and can read balanced-equation coefficients as mole ratios.
  1. On a 100 g basis for 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O, convert each mass to amount before comparing the elements.
    Why valid: Formula subscripts follow relative atom amounts, not mass percentages.
    Result: Approximately 3.33 mol C : 6.65 mol H : 3.33 mol O.
  2. Divide by the smallest amount and scale only if needed to obtain the smallest reliable whole-number ratio.
    Why valid: A common scaling preserves the relative particle counts represented by the empirical formula.
    Result: The ratio is approximately 1 : 2 : 1, giving empirical formula CH₂O.
  3. For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O with 3.0 mol H₂ and 2.0 mol O₂, place the initial mole inventories beside the balanced coefficients.
    Why valid: Stoichiometric coefficients compare amounts of substance, so the inventories must be expressed in moles.
    Result: H₂ supports 3.0/2 = 1.5 reaction extents; O₂ supports 2.0/1 = 2.0.
  4. Choose the smaller supported extent and use it to compute consumption, product and leftover amounts.
    Why valid: The first exhausted reactant fixes the maximum complete-reaction extent under the model.
    Result: H₂ is limiting; 3.0 mol H₂ and 1.5 mol O₂ are consumed, 3.0 mol H₂O forms, and 0.5 mol O₂ remains.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
ξi=niνi;ξlimiting=min(ξi)

A balanced equation compares amounts through stoichiometric coefficients; the reactant supporting the smallest reaction extent is limiting in the complete-reaction model.

  • Convert each starting inventory to moles first.
  • Use a balanced equation and the complete-reaction stoichiometric model.

Watch one

For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, a mixture starts with 3.0 mol H₂ and 2.0 mol O₂. Use ni/νi to identify the limiting reactant, then determine H₂O formed and excess O₂ remaining.

  1. For H₂, ni/νi = 3.0/2 = 1.5.
  2. For O₂, ni/νi = 2.0/1 = 2.0.
  3. The smaller supported extent, 1.5, makes H₂ limiting.
  4. At extent 1.5, product H₂O = 2 × 1.5 = 3.0 mol and O₂ consumed = 1 × 1.5 = 1.5 mol, leaving 0.5 mol O₂.

Result

H₂ is limiting; 3.0 mol H₂O forms and 0.5 mol O₂ remains.

Check

Confirm that the calculated consumption of neither reactant exceeds its initial inventory.

Formula subscripts and balanced-equation coefficients are particle/mole ratios, so mass data must first be converted to amount before formula or limiting-reagent decisions are made.

A tempting wrong path

  • The largest mass percentage gives the largest formula subscript, and the reactant with the smaller starting mass is always limiting.

Diagnose

  • Can a light element contribute many atoms but little mass, and can different molar masses make the smaller gram amount contain more moles?

Repair

  • Convert mass to moles before both formula-ratio and limiting-reagent comparisons.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckWhy must percentage or mass data be converted to moles before formula subscripts are inferred?
  2. 2 · ApplyFor 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O with 3.0 mol H₂ and 2.0 mol O₂, compare ni/νi and identify the limiting reactant.
  3. 3 · ConnectOnce reaction inventories are in moles, connect them to concentration data only through the correct concentration denominator.

Molarity, molality and mole fraction

Hard concept

The key step

Molarity, molality and mole fraction normalize composition differently; a temperature change can alter a volume-based ratio even when the chemical composition of a closed sample is unchanged.

You need

  • Can identify numerator and denominator in a ratio and can distinguish solution volume from solvent mass and total mole amount.
  1. Write the denominator first: solution volume for molarity, solvent mass for molality, total moles for mole fraction.
    Why valid: These concentration measures are not interchangeable because they normalize composition differently.
    Result: The denominator determines units and temperature sensitivity.
  2. For temperature-change reasoning, state whether the sample is closed and composition/amounts remain unchanged.
    Why valid: Molarity can change if solution volume changes; molality and mole fraction do not change merely from temperature when solvent mass and mole amounts stay unchanged.
    Result: Closed unchanged sample: Vsolution ↑ with fixed nsolute ⇒ c = n/V ↓, while fixed msolvent and fixed mole amounts leave b and xi unchanged.
  3. Identify exactly which numerator or denominator changes in the scenario.
    Why valid: A generic claim that 'temperature changes concentration' hides the definition that controls the result.
    Result: Definition-level bookkeeping closes the reasoning.

Equations and validity

EquationWhat it tells youWhen you can use it
c=nsoluteVsolution;b=nsolutemsolvent

Molarity, molality and mole fraction use different denominators, so their units and temperature sensitivity follow different bookkeeping.

  • State whether the sample is closed and whether composition changes.
  • Use solution volume for molarity, solvent mass for molality and total mole amount for mole fraction.

Watch one

Why can molarity change with temperature even when no solute or solvent is added or removed?

  1. A closed unchanged sample can keep the solute mole amount constant.
  2. Thermal expansion or contraction can change the solution-volume denominator.
  3. The ratio therefore can change without a composition change.

Result

Because molarity divides solute amount by solution volume, and the volume can change with temperature while the solute amount remains fixed.

Check

Name which numerator or denominator changed rather than attributing the result generically to temperature.

Molarity, molality and mole fraction normalize composition differently; a temperature change can alter a volume-based ratio even when the chemical composition of a closed sample is unchanged.

A tempting wrong path

  • Every concentration measure must change with temperature because physical samples expand or contract.

Diagnose

  • In a sealed unchanged sample whose solution volume changes with temperature, which denominators change directly: solution volume, solvent mass or total mole amount?

Repair

  • Follow the definition and state the closure/composition assumptions before making a temperature-dependence claim.

1-2-3 quick check

  1. 1 · CheckName the denominator used by molarity, molality and mole fraction.
  2. 2 · ApplyA sealed solution warms and expands without loss or reaction. Identify which of c, b and xi can change from the definitions alone.
  3. 3 · ConnectUse denominator-first bookkeeping when concentration becomes an input to later stoichiometric calculations.
